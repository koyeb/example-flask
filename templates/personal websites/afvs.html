AFVS 97: A Pig's Death & A Pig's Funeral

Two linked studio projects on gaze, identity, and code — built with wireframe, clay, and projected distortion running through Processing and p5.js.

Course: AFVS 97
Media: Sculpture + facial-tracking projection
Tools: Processing, p5.js, Python

Project 1 — A Pig's Death

I have always been interested in the general themes of rebirth, death, gaze, and the grotesque, and the intertwining play of execution. This project set out to be more personal and exploratory as I tried to grasp all of my past identities, beliefs, and hobbies and put them on display like a public execution — I was called a pig (non-offensively), I am a STEM major, I like drag and dramatic make-up, et cetera et cetera. As I tried to learn more about myself, I wanted to try out new media: wireframe and clay, plus a mix of discarded materials I was able to find. There are two big sections of this piece: the head and the projection.

Snapshots of the pig's head in progress — wireframe, foil, then paint and discarded materials.

I started out very interested in performance art and the uncanny, almost anonymously voyeuristic feeling of public execution. Attacking the theme of a view of not my world taught me a lot about logistics and physics to build a back-facing headpiece, which naturally led to my decision not to use the sculpture on a moving body. Going into this project either without any expectation or with too ambitious ones, I learned to navigate the trade-offs between a medium and its implied values — there were times I didn't like how a medium turned out and had to reverse-engineer everything.

Draft code → actual projection.

Besides the struggle of experimenting with a new medium, my biggest challenge was how to approach mixed media without "doing too much" or losing the desired effect. I got lucky without needing multiple prototypes and still landed the face distortion effect I wanted, but the hesitation around it was a real mental barrier — most of this project's friction came from not being able to move past my own what-ifs.

I actually regretted that this project couldn't be exhibited as a moving piece — not for technical reasons, but because I couldn't figure out how to keep it stable on a moving body, and a lot of the head's components were wobbly enough to fall off mid-motion. A more visual-striking, moving sculpture is my biggest "what if" for this piece — though it would have lost the layer of altered reality and identity reconstruction that only works as a static projected sculpture.

360° sculpture at studio.

What I'd still ask

My original idea was to figure out the dynamics of the gazed and the gazer, as I embrace myself as the gazed: I cannot see the world — the world is seeing what I cannot see — what I cannot see is seeing the world. How do I strengthen that relationship further? How many questions and references can a project hold before it becomes too saturated? Should I have left out the feminine skin/hair as a reference to the male gaze, since it might draw attention away?

Sculpture at home with projection.

Project 2 — A Pig's Funeral

This project is guided by an ongoing question that began in A Pig's Death: what does it mean to be seen? What is an unstable gaze, a partial gaze, or an absent gaze? For this second project, I expanded that into a spatial and participatory system — building an enclosed booth that separates the viewer (outside) from the viewed (inside), using red fabric, netting, and a suspended structure that references both a body and an interior organ. I let the material stay visibly constructed and unstable, embracing the "wobbly," fragmented quality I'd previously resisted.

The installation runs through three states: when someone is inside the booth looking, when they're inside but not looking, and when the booth is empty.

Experiment with the code (Python + Processing) in another space — the booth's three states.

Early experiments focused on facial tracking and distortion, similar to the projection in the pig's head, but simply distorting the face felt too literal. A key shift happened when I tested what occurs when no eyes are detected — that became the second state, where the body dissolves into a negative space filled with text from my queer diaries. Closing my eyes meant seeing neither myself nor any color, so I leaned into the inner thoughts instead, translating them into text so identity could be replaced with memory and interiority. The third state, when no one is present, extends the first project's "what if" about autonomy — the system keeps running as a kind of memory capsule, suggesting identity persists even without a viewer.

One of the most important developments was the booth itself. What started as a neutral, mechanically difficult photo booth turned into a womb-like structure, referencing gynecological imagery and the idea of the gaze as a forceful birth. Its openings invite outside viewers to look in, yet the orientation of the viewed's posture prevents any direct eye contact — the same misalignment I explored in A Pig's Death. The pig's head, reframed within a "funeral," becomes both a remnant and a continuation, linking death to distortion to a sense of rebirth.

Position of the projected frame vs. the booth.

The frame as trigger

Another important component is the projection onto the frame. The frame functions less as a neutral display device and more as a conceptual trigger: it signals to participants that what appears within it is meant to be looked at, evaluated, treated as "art." Placing the live projection of the person inside the booth within a framed boundary intentionally collapses the distinction between subject and artwork — entering the booth already asks for a level of commitment, and the frame intensifies that awareness. Even before stepping inside, participants understand their presence will be translated, distorted, and displayed to an outside audience. In that sense, the frame quietly asks: are you willing to be seen like this?

I'm still questioning whether the work invites genuine self-reflection, or if the mix of overstimulating elements overrides that possibility — a tension between being seen, seeing oneself, and existing without either, and a question of the gaze's technological moment now versus what it becomes further down the human timeline.
